<section className="pg-section">
<div className="pg-sectionhead">
<h2>One rollout, end to end</h2>
<p>In September 2026 we introduced separate Development and Production versions for Next-Gen Applications, a change to how customers build and publish their work. Applications in Classic mode were not affected. I ran release planning for it and owned the communication around it.</p>
</div>

<div className="pg-split">
<div>
<span className="pg-eyebrow">What I drove</span>

<ul className="pg-list">
<li><strong>Ran release planning.</strong> I ran the planning meeting that limited the general release to bug fixes and minor, non-impacting features, agreed with Product and Engineering, then wrote up the decisions and held everyone to them.</li>
<li><strong>Made the gate explicit.</strong> I named two engineering leads as gatekeepers and sent every late addition to them for regression testing. I also froze the customer communication list until deployment.</li>
<li><strong>Rolled out in two stages.</strong> Our internal environments and four customer environments moved on 19 September, and every other customer environment on 26 September. I confirmed the first group of customers and their contacts.</li>
<li><strong>Set the deadlines.</strong> A content deadline for product managers in the Release Hub, and a deadline for engineering to tag every ticket in the release.</li>
<li><strong>Wrote the one-way rule into the docs.</strong> Deploying an application to Production locks it to Next-Gen mode. I wrote the user message for it and added it to the FAQ and the release note.</li>
<li><strong>Kept two messages apart.</strong> The internal briefing stayed separate from what customers read, and the product's own terms matched the interface.</li>
</ul>

<div className="pg-result">The rollout went out in two stages on the dates set. Customer Success was briefed before the first stage, and the customer email went out before the full rollout.</div>
</div>

<h2>The platform roadmap</h2>
<p>Leadership, sales and Customer Success all needed one answer to "what is coming, and when" across three product areas. I rebuilt the roadmap from scratch: the process product managers follow, the automation that keeps it current, and the dashboard everyone reads.</p>
</div>

<div className="pg-grid-3" style={{marginTop: '1.8rem'}}>
<div className="pg-card">
<span className="pg-tag">Process</span>

<h3>Four fields, set by the people who own the work</h3>

Only epics go on the roadmap, and a product manager puts one there by setting four Jira fields: a yes/no switch for whether it belongs on the roadmap, the quarter it is planned for, a roadmap status that is separate from the engineering workflow status, and a one-line update in plain English. An optional customer summary line in the description is what sales and Customer Success see. I wrote the rules, sent them to every product manager, and chase the updates each month and at every quarter close.
</div>

<div className="pg-card">
<span className="pg-tag">Automation</span>

<h3>Live from Jira, checked every morning</h3>

The dashboard reads the Jira-fed sheets every time it opens, so there is no manual refresh and no copy that goes stale. Every weekday morning a Claude job compares the sheets with Jira field by field, lists epics that were added or dropped, and sends me what disagrees. One command brings the sheets back in line.
</div>

<div className="pg-card">
<span className="pg-tag">Dashboard</span>

<h3>One view for three product areas</h3>

Now, Next and Earlier tabs, the current and next quarter as one-click filters, product areas down the side, a status badge and the plain-English update on every row, and search by key or name. There is a separate customer-facing version for sales. Leadership uses the internal view in its weekly report, and the sales team has presented from it at a customer's site.
</div>
</div>

<section className="pg-section">
<div className="pg-sectionhead">
<h2>Sprint checkpoints</h2>
<p>Product managers had many recurring commitments and no single list of them. I designed one, and I run it.</p>
</div>

<div className="pg-split">
<div>

Eight checkpoints across every two-week sprint. Each has an owner, a definition of what "done" means, the source to update, and a reminder I post in the product managers' channel. I took the product managers' feedback on what to merge and what happens when a sprint slips. The work then moved toward making Jira the single source of truth, so the reminders point at one place.

</div>

<h2>Product and Customer Success, agreed before release</h2>
<p>Customer Success had no single place to see what was coming in a release. I put two steps in front of every release.</p>
</div>

<div className="pg-grid-3">
<div className="pg-card">
<h3>A preview a week ahead</h3>

The Release Hub, published about a week before each release, sorted by what customers must do. Product managers get a two-day review window to confirm their rows. <a href="/ai">How it is built</a>.
</div>

<div className="pg-card">
<h3>A pre-release session</h3>

Product managers walk Customer Success through the release before it ships, and I follow up on what changes. It now runs before each release.
</div>

<div className="pg-card">
<h3>Docs planned at release planning</h3>

A single planning list became the source of truth for the docs in each release. Anything not raised at planning is not planned for, except a genuine urgent addition. I also asked a team that kept missing release meetings to send someone.
</div>
